Overview

MTU-Bench asks a deceptively simple question about tool use: does a model's competence survive as the interaction grows more complicated? It probes the same underlying skill at four granularities — single-turn and multi-turn dialogue crossed with single-tool and multi-tool calls — and adds out-of-distribution tasks on top. This page adopts the multi-turn multi-tool (M-M) setting as the primary leaderboard: it is the hardest of the four and the closest proxy for genuine tool orchestration.

Two design choices set it apart from earlier tool-use suites. Scoring is entirely objective — every metric is computed against ground truth, with no GPT-based judge and no human raters — which keeps evaluation cheap, reproducible, and stable at scale. And instead of authoring scenarios from scratch, the benchmark transforms existing high-quality tasks into tool-use episodes, which broadens coverage. It ships alongside MTU-Instruct, a companion resource for improving the very abilities the benchmark measures.

The four settings are kept separate rather than averaged. The paper reports no single unified overall score, so any aggregate would be an editorial invention; the M-M column serves as the headline number here, while the other three granularities remain on the page to explain a ranking — to show whether a model earns its position on hard orchestration or on easier single-turn calls.

Why it matters

Tool calling is the substrate of agent products, and the failure that matters in deployment is rarely one malformed call — it is losing the thread across turns while juggling several tools at once. By grading each granularity separately, MTU-Bench turns a vague label like “good at tools” into a profile, showing where competence gives out as state and choice multiply.

The objective scoring matters just as much. Judge-model evaluation inherits the judge's biases along with its price; ground-truth metrics make the benchmark inexpensive enough to run routinely and consistent enough to compare across labs. For anyone choosing a model to sit inside an agent framework, that reproducibility is the point.

Contributions

  • M-M is the hardest setting and best reflects complex tool orchestration ability.

Method & evaluation

  • MTU-Bench evaluates tool-use ability while emphasizing differences across multiple interaction granularities.
  • It covers single-turn and multi-turn settings, single-tool and multi-tool settings, as well as out-of-distribution tasks.
  • Unlike many prior benchmarks, it avoids GPT-based or human scoring and uses objective metrics derived from ground truth.
  • The benchmark is built by transforming existing high-quality tasks and is paired with MTU-Instruct for improvement.
  • This makes it both broader in coverage and cheaper to evaluate at scale.

Reading the results

Qwen-Max leads the M-M leaderboard at 45.08, with GPT-4 at 41.36 and GLM-4-Plus at 39.53 — a top cluster spanning just 5.6 points. Below it the table falls away: DeepSeek-V2.5 sits 8.9 points behind third place at 30.62, and GPT-3.5 Turbo trails the leader by 33.2 points at 11.87. No model reaches even half of the 0–100 range, which says less about any one system than about the setting itself — multi-turn multi-tool orchestration remains mostly unsolved.

Two within-family gaps sharpen the picture. Alibaba's Qwen-Max outstrips Qwen2-72B-Instruct by almost 20 points, and OpenAI's GPT-4 sits 29.5 points above GPT-3.5 Turbo — nearly the full 33.2-point spread of the table. On this evidence, multi-turn multi-tool competence tracks model generation and tier far more than provider, and the ceiling leaves every system with a majority of the range unclaimed.
