Overview

Nearly every video benchmark hands a model one clip and asks questions about it. MVU-Eval breaks that assumption: each task requires holding several videos in view at once — comparing what they show, retrieving the relevant one, or reasoning over evidence scattered across them. The benchmark's premise, argued directly by its authors, is that real workloads routinely demand this kind of joint analysis, and single-video evaluation never touches it.

Coverage spans both perception and reasoning: object recognition, spatial understanding, knowledge-intensive reasoning, and retrieval-augmented tasks in which the useful evidence must first be found among the inputs. Everything rolls up into a single Overall accuracy on a 0-100 scale — the same column this leaderboard reports, taken from the paper's main table without recombining the perception and reasoning subtasks.

Why it matters

MVU-Eval's authors report that multi-video settings significantly separate model capabilities — the format itself is diagnostic, not merely harder. What it isolates is cross-instance integration: carrying objects, spatial relations, and facts from one clip into a question that spans another. Any workload that hands a model several videos rather than one is implicitly betting on that capability, and single-video benchmarks offer no way to price the bet.

Method & evaluation

  • This work argues that existing video benchmarks mostly assume single-video input, while real tasks often require joint analysis over multiple videos.
  • MVU-Eval covers both perception and reasoning, including object recognition, spatial understanding, knowledge-intensive reasoning, and retrieval-augmented tasks.
  • The results show that multi-video settings significantly separate model capabilities, highlighting the need for stronger cross-instance integration.
