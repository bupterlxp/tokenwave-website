Overview

Most instruction-following benchmarks reward models for doing what they were trained to do. Inverse IFEval asks the opposite question: when a user's instruction conflicts with the conventions a model absorbed during training, does the model follow the user or the habit? Its tasks are built around deliberately counter-conventional demands — corrections, formatting that violates learned defaults, mid-dialogue interventions, and requirements to change as little as possible.

The design goes beyond checking formatting compliance. Each inverse instruction is constructed to pull against a training convention, so passing requires the model to recognize that the user's stated intent overrides its priors. Performance is summarized as an Overall Score on a 0-100 scale; the leaderboard here reports the English main table rather than recombining English and Chinese results.

The headline finding is uncomfortable for anyone who assumed instruction following was solved: even the strongest models show clear weaknesses once instructions turn inverse. Compliance with familiar formats, it turns out, is not the same skill as obedience under conflict — and only the second one generalizes to users whose needs do not match the training distribution.

Why it matters

Deployed models constantly meet users whose requirements cut against defaults: formatting that breaks convention, a correction issued mid-conversation, an edit that must change as little as possible. A model that reverts to its training habits in those moments is not following instructions; it is autocompleting them. Inverse IFEval isolates exactly that failure mode, one that conventional compliance suites are structurally unable to see.

Method & evaluation

  • This work goes beyond conventional formatting compliance and explicitly designs inverse instructions that conflict with common training conventions.
  • The tasks test whether models can still obey real user intent under correction, counter-conventional formatting, dialogue intervention, and minimal-modification requirements.
  • The results show that even the strongest models still have clear weaknesses on inverse instruction following.

Reading the results

OpenAI holds the entire podium: o3-high leads at 75.66, with o3-mini just 0.99 behind and GPT-5-high at 73.72 — three systems inside a two-point band. Gemini 2.5 Pro follows at 70.55, and Claude 4 Opus Thinking closes the table at 67.16, an 8.5-point spread across six models. The compression at the top matters less than the ceiling: the best model still leaves roughly a quarter of the 0-100 range on the table, so training habits are being broken, but not reliably.
