Models follow instructions well — until the instruction contradicts their training prior. Inverse IFEval measures counter-intuitive instruction following, where compliance requires overriding the likely answer.

Tell a model to answer in JSON and it answers in JSON. Tell it to write the buggy version of a function, to argue the unpopular side, to format things the "wrong" way — and something interesting happens: the training prior pushes back. Inverse IFEval is a benchmark made entirely of that push-back.

Obedience is easy until it costs something

Standard instruction-following suites measure formatting compliance along the grain of what models already want to produce. Inverse IFEval designs instructions that conflict with common training conventions, so every point scored is evidence the model can place the user's actual request above its own habits. That is not a stylistic skill — it is the load-bearing trust property of any system that edits, red-teams, or role-plays on command.

Deliberate-reasoning models cluster at the top, which fits the mechanism: overriding a prior seems to require noticing the prior first, and models with an explicit thinking phase have somewhere to do that noticing. Yet the ceiling sits near 76 — a quarter of counter-intuitive instructions still lose to habit, even for the best systems.

An assistant that only follows instructions it already agrees with is not following instructions at all.

We consider this one of the most safety-adjacent capability numbers we track: the same override machinery that writes intentionally bad code on request is what keeps a model steerable when its defaults are wrong.
